# Research synthesis

**Katherine Lin · The Listening Room**

## Research question

How can an older listener return to a familiar song when the phone interface or the search tool interrupts the routine?

I used experience-centred design to examine what people were doing before a breakdown, how it affected the moment and what they did next. Familiar songs mattered because they accompanied ordinary activities and carried emotional associations. The design question was how to restore access with minimal additional effort.

## Evidence and method

I reviewed literature on digital confidence, music experiences and voice interaction, and compared five music services during desk research. I conducted two remote interviews with older adults. I used a semi-structured guide, transcribed the conversations and coded observations by routine, breakdown, workaround and desired control. The design targets adults aged 65 and over, a broader audience than the two firsthand interviews.

The project archive supplies additional qualitative context and an early walkthrough of the phone and tactile controller. I used recurring qualitative signals to form design hypotheses. The appendix materials mix working profiles, drafts and repeated accounts, so the public synthesis uses the two traceable firsthand interviews for its sample count. Reported voice and interface difficulties reflect lived experiences with existing products. The newer prototype requires usability validation with intended listeners.

## Research themes and design responses

| Observation | Why it matters | Design response |
|---|---|---|
| Familiar music accompanies everyday activities. Difficulties accessing saved music can interrupt those routines. | The cost of navigation failure includes the loss of the moment. | Put recent playback and saved music on Home and Library. Preserve the session in a mini-player. |
| Mis-taps and changes to familiar controls create uncertainty. | Relearning controls creates friction and can discourage experimentation. | Keep tab destinations stable, label playback controls and offer undo after saving or removing. |
| Accent sensitivity and exact-title matching make it harder to retrieve a known song. | A convenient input can become an extra barrier when the system guesses wrong. | Design cancel, retry, match review and typed fallback as first-class routes. |
| The research pointed to familiar, stable controls with clear labels. | Daily control should be easy to recognise through touch or sight. | Give MusicTag a bounded set of transport and volume controls. Explain its role during setup. |
| Sharing music can encourage conversation. Links can add steps to a shared listening experience. | The social value depends on the ease of getting to the music. | Keep shared playlists in the library and make recipient permissions explicit. |

## Affinity themes

- **Ritual and memory.** Familiar music filled quiet moments, accompanied chores or journeys, and recalled people and events.
- **Control and confidence.** A crowded screen, small target, unexpected change or failed device connection could break a familiar sequence.
- **Workarounds.** Listeners returned to known songs or familiar devices, asked for help selectively, or put the music aside.
- **Preferred interaction.** Stable layouts, visible wording, voice that accommodates variation and practical setup instructions reduced the perceived effort of trying.
- **Connection across generations.** Family listening and shared playlists created opportunities for conversation when access was straightforward.

## Design personas

These are fictional composites used to evaluate flows.

| Persona | Routine and goal | Design pressure |
|---|---|---|
| **Mary**, routine listener | Returns to familiar songs at home and enjoys selections from family. | Finds her usual music with a stable layout and a clearly labelled recovery action when search misses. |
| **Bill**, tactile-first listener | Wants to control playback while occupied and prefers visible wording and physical feedback. | Recognises play, skip and volume and understands where to choose a new playlist. |
| **Heritage-music listener**, voice discovery lens | Uses familiar artists and titles across languages. | Can recover from transliteration, accent and ambiguous-match problems without having to restate a query repeatedly. |

## What this evidence supports

The research supports prioritising familiar music, predictable controls and recovery. It also identifies recognition across accents, usability with vision or dexterity differences, and comprehension of the phone–remote split as risks to test. It provides qualitative direction for this brief. It does not establish the prevalence of the problems or a measurable effect on confidence or loneliness.

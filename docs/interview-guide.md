# Interview guide and fieldwork summary

**Katherine Lin · MusicTag**

## Moderator opening

Thank the participant and explain that the conversation explores music in daily life. Explain how notes or recordings will be used, confirm consent before recording, and remind the participant that they can skip a question or stop. Ask about a recent experience before introducing the design idea.

## Interview questions

| Topic | Main question | Neutral follow-up |
|---|---|---|
| Listening routine | Tell me about the last time you listened to music. | Where were you and what were you doing? |
| Music and memory | Tell me about a song that brings a person or moment to mind. | What happens for you when it comes on? |
| Device and context | How do you usually get music playing? | Show or describe the steps you take. |
| Friction | Tell me about a time those steps did not work as expected. | What did you try next? |
| Comfort and confidence | What helps you feel comfortable using a music app or device by yourself? | What would make the experience harder? |
| Sharing | Tell me about the last time you shared or received a song. | What did you or the recipient do with it? |
| Voice | Have you tried asking a device to find music? | What did you say and how did it respond? |
| Ideal experience | Imagine listening works the way you want. How would it work? | Which part would you want to stay predictable? |
| Close | What else should I understand about the way you listen? | Is there something important I have missed? |

## Themes from the fieldwork

The interview materials informed four design themes: predictable access to familiar music, visible and stable controls, speech recovery, and easier sharing. The research included different listening frequencies and comfort levels with mobile apps and physical controls. These themes shaped the interview questions above and the fictional design personas.

## Synthesis procedure

1. Code each observation as context, goal, friction, workaround or desired behaviour.
2. Cluster similar observations and retain disconfirming details.
3. Preserve the chain from an observed event to a design hypothesis, such as a failed search leading to an immediate typed fallback.
4. Translate the chains into tasks a reviewer can perform.
5. Mark uncertainties for the next evaluation, especially accent coverage, mixed-language queries and the physical controls.

The public field notes use paraphrase and remove identifying details. Original recordings, raw transcripts and consent materials are private research records.
